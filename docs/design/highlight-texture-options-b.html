What should the highlighter's ink look like, and what happens where two marks overlap?

Five shapes for the mark, four textures for its ink, six rules for what happens where one mark lands inside another, and three that only make sense moving — all on the same real page (page 42, the Throne Verse, 2:255, inside the passage 2:254 to 2:256), at the width a phone gives the page.

This page on the site: blog.bytesofpurpose.com/hifth/docs/design/highlight-texture-options-b.html

The short version
  1. Every picture is the same page. The shapes and textures change only the verse's own mark; the overlap rules are each drawn twice — a verse inside a passage, and a run of words inside the verse.
  2. Look at arm's length first (can you find it?), then close (can you still read the letters and their vowel marks?). The live ones at the bottom need a press.
  3. Under each picture: how it is made, and measured contrast — the letters on the mark (4.5:1 is the reading floor) and the mark against the paper (3:1 is the floor for a "you are here" sign). For overlaps, also the inner mark against the outer one: can you tell them apart?
  4. The pros, the cons, what each commits us to, and a recommendation are in the record this page belongs to.
What is already settled. A reader may choose among the swipe, a fill and an outline, and tune strength in a few named steps (2 September). Marks are drawn per line, never one box around a whole verse. This page does not re-ask either; every shape here is still one mark per line.

Contrast figures are computed from the app's own colour values (print ink #231f20, paper #fbf8f2, amber #e8a13a, verdigris #1f6f66), not sampled from screenshots. Letters on plain paper read at 15.4:1. For a texture, the figure given is the worst point of it — the lightest speck, the dry end — which is what the web accessibility guideline asks for when a background varies. If a device asks for more contrast, this page drops every filter and the ink goes flat; if it asks for less motion, nothing wipes or moves.
