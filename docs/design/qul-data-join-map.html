The pieces of the outside library of Qur'an data, how they clip together, where their keys line up and where they do not, and what each one is for. This page holds no Qur'an text — every column of Arabic script is named and counted, never shown.

Why this page exists. The app now holds a copy of parts of this library in a store it controls, off the code and out of what ships. We decided what that copy is for: to draw our own word-by-word page of the Qur'an and stand it beside the printed page the app already shows, to check our page is right — and to feed the look-alike-verse work its numbers. This is the picture that decision was made on. The reasons live in the decision record, what the held copy is for. Now loaded. As of 8 September 2026 the two held pieces are in that store and checked there: the page plan (9,046 lines across 604 pages) and the word text (83,668 words), their licences cleared. Each line-range resolves to real words, with no gaps and nothing dangling. The two fonts are cleared to hold but are not loaded yet.

Words a stranger would need:
  • Ayah — a verse. There are 6,236 of them.
  • Mus'haf — the printed Qur'an, 604 pages, 15 lines to a page.
  • Word-by-word — the text broken into single words, each in its own place, the way a page is really set.
  • Word id — a single 1-to-83,668 number given to every word in reading order. This is the backbone.
  • Verse key — "surah:ayah", e.g. the 23rd ayah of surah 2 is written 2:23.
  • Root / lemma / stem — three ways of naming the linguistic core a word is built from.

What ties these files together?

One backbone, and three ways things hang off it. The backbone is the word id: every word in the book, numbered 1 to 83,668 in reading order. The page plan hangs off it from above (each line owns a run of word ids), the word text sits right on it, the linguistics hang off to one side (keyed by where each word sits — surah, ayah, word), and everything about whole verses hangs off the other side (keyed by the verse). The fonts sit underneath, drawing what the plan and the text place.

How does drawing our own page check the printed one?

The app shows the mus'haf as outlined artwork — the printed page drawn as shapes, with no letters in it. That is what readers see, and it stays that way. The trouble is that the artwork looks like a page whether or not it is the right page: if the wrong page were shown for a verse, or a line break fell in the wrong place, nothing in the building tools would catch it. The only check today is a person holding a printed copy against the screen.

The held copy gives the building tools a second witness. From the page plan and the word text we can draw our own word-by-word page — the same 15 lines, the same words in the same places — and lay it beside the artwork the app ships. If the two disagree about which verse a page carries or where a line turns, the drawing catches it. The drawing never ships; it exists so the shipped page can be checked by something other than a human with a printed mus'haf.

What ships

The printed page, as outlined artwork — no letters, the reader's view. Unchanged.

What checks it

Our own page, drawn word-by-word from the held copy, stood beside it in the building tools. Disagreements are registration mistakes to fix.

This is a proofing tool, the way publishers proof a new edition — laid page-for-page beside a trusted one. It is not a second thing readers see, and it does not put Qur'an text into what ships.

Where do the keys NOT line up?

Word id and the page plan: perfect. Both run 1..83,668 with no gaps. Drawing a page is just "pull the words whose id falls in this line's range."
The linguistics are thinner than the text, on purpose. Of 83,668 words, a root is known for 50,298 (60%), a lemma for 72,510 (87%), a stem for 77,427 (93%). Not every word is built on a triliteral root, so this is language, not damage. Every linguistics row points at a real word — zero dangling pointers.
Two "look-alike verse" sets, counting different things. One is 3,552 scored verse-to-verse pairs over 1,162 source verses. The other is phrase-anchored: 814 phrases spread across 2,232 verses. They agree in spirit but are not the same measurement — the pairwise-scored set is the stronger independent cross-check for the look-alike work the app already ships.

The licence reality: the library prints no licence label on any of these resource pages. Its own guidance says licensing is per-resource and varies (public domain / attribution / restricted) and tells you to check each originator's own terms. Three originators to clear before anything they made may be held, all attribution-bearing and some commercial-restricted upstream: King Fahd Complex (the word text and fonts), DigitalKhatt / Dr. Amin Anane (the page plan), Dr. Kais Dukes / Tanzil (the linguistics and base text). Until an item's terms are read, that item is not held — the drawing is built only from what has cleared.
