Hifth · trust

How we earned your trust

We don't ask you to take our word for it.

Hifth carries the Qur'an, marks where you slip, and shows you the verses you are likely to confuse. For every one of those things, we found someone outside this project who had built the same thing on their own — and we compared, letter by letter and page by page. This is what we checked, what we found, and — plainly — where we still fall short.

Why should an app that carries the Qur'an have to prove itself?

Because a muṣḥaf is not a place for "trust me." A wrong page number, a mislaid mark, a confusion the app failed to warn you about — any of these quietly teaches a mistake to someone memorising. So the rule this project holds itself to is simple: nothing the app shows you rests on our own say-so.

Everything is built up from the printed page. And for each thing we build, we look for an independent witness — a separate group who built the same thing without ever seeing our work — and we check ourselves against them. When we agree, we lock the check so it runs again every single time the app is rebuilt. When we differ, we look at every difference and explain each one. And when a witness turns out not to be truly independent after all, we say so and stop leaning on it.

So — what did the witnesses say?

Six of the checks that matter most to a reader. Each one names what we built, who we checked it against, and the number that came back.

holds

Is every verse on the page it is truly on?

568 / 604pages agree — and the 36 that don't are the ones the printings themselves disagree on

The app knows which page every verse sits on by reading the shape of the printed page directly. To check it, we compared our page for every verse against a widely used, separately maintained page list that has never seen our artwork.

568 of the 604 pages match exactly. The 36 that differ are not errors: they are precisely the pages where the two standard printings of this muṣḥaf are known to break differently. A second, independent reference agreed with us on 6,180 of the 6,236 verses, with every one of the differences falling in those same known places.

honest gap

Does the app catch the verses you would actually confuse?

41%of a much larger phrase catalogue — and that is the number we wanted, not a shortfall

Our list of look-alikes is hand-picked: the verses a hafiz genuinely slips between. We measured it against a far larger catalogue, built by others, that mechanically lists every repeated phrase in the Qur'an — 2,232 verses in all.

We flag 921 of those 2,232 verses. The two lists are deliberately not the same: they hold 1,311 verses we leave out, and we flag 599 they never list at all. That is by design — they catalogue every mechanical echo, including formulas that recur in dozens of verses, while we keep only the ones that actually trip a reader. A near-perfect match would have been the worrying result, not this one: it would suggest we had simply copied them.

Being honest about the gap: of the 1,311 we don't flag, 677 sit in a tight, two-to-four verse echo — real candidates worth a second look (verses like 2:8, 2:25, 2:30 among them). The other 634 are the long, common refrains we leave alone on purpose. The list is not finished — but it is not thin, and it is nobody's copy.

holds

Do the recitation colours come from somewhere we can stand behind?

99.80%agreement with a separate engine that carries no Qur'an text of its own

The colours over the letters mark the rules of tajweed. We checked them against an independently built recitation engine that ships no verse data at all — it works the rules out for itself. Across all 6,236 verses it agrees with our colouring 99.80% of the time, and every one of the 108 differences is written down for a hafiz to judge.

And a caution we owe you plainly: a different source we looked at, which seemed to be a second independent witness, turned out to be the same underlying data — its rule counts matched ours exactly, down to the rarest rule appearing on the very same 13 verses. We did not count it twice. A witness only counts when it is genuinely its own.

holds

Is the printed page really the one we say it is?

56 / 56pages confirm it is the exact printing we claim

Tapping a single word on the page relies on a second, independent scan of the muṣḥaf. Before trusting it, we had to be sure it was the same printing we ship — not a lookalike edition that breaks pages differently. So we checked 56 pages, chosen to include the handful where the two standard printings are famously different, against our own page list.

All 56 matched. Picking exactly the pages that would betray a wrong edition is what makes this a real test and not a lucky coincidence.

holds

Are the fixed facts of the book actually right?

318structural numbers, re-checked against their source on every single build

How many verses are in each chapter; where each of the thirty parts begins; where every place of prostration falls — 318 fixed numbers in all. These are carried in the app's own code, which is exactly the kind of place a typo hides.

So they are not trusted — they are re-derived from the reference reckoning of the book every time the app is built, and compared. A single mistaken digit stops the build. This is the check we hold up as the model for all the others.

holds

Does every small mark sit on a word that calls for it?

86,962 / 86,965words carry exactly the marks their text asks for

The tiny marks over the words — the pause signs, the elongations — were counted, word by word, against what the text of each word actually calls for. All but three of the 86,965 words match exactly. Whether the print or the text is right about those three is a hafiz's call, and it is set aside for one — not swept away.

What can no computer ever check?

Some things a machine simply cannot settle. A comparison can tell you two verses share words; it cannot tell you a reader would confuse them. For those, the only honest witness is a person, and we keep a standing list of exactly which checks still need one — and which have been done.

  • □Whether a hafiz, holding a printed muṣḥaf, agrees that our look-alike pairs are ones you would really confuse. Waiting on a hafiz.
  • □Whether a screen-reader user can actually select a verse and hop between look-alikes by touch, and whether it is phrased well by ear. Waiting on a phone and an ear.
  • □Whether your revision history really survives a real phone left offline for more than a week. Waiting on a real device and real days.
  • ✓Whether a stranger, logged out, can reach the app's open source code. Done.
  • ✓How far a mark still sits from where it belongs, measured by placing marks on the page by hand. Done.
Two of these are finished; the rest are honestly still open. We would rather show you an unfinished list than pretend a person has looked when no person has.

Where did we come up short?

Trust is not a clean sheet — it is knowing exactly where the edges are. As of today:

  • ▸The look-alike list is not finished. There are 677 verse pairings, tight enough to be worth flagging, that we do not yet flag.
  • ▸We have not settled which words to underline when two verses share an opening. There are two defensible answers and the choice is still open.
  • ▸The 108 tajweed differences still need a hafiz. We have found and listed every one; none has yet been judged.
  • ▸One licence we still read secondhand. The printing authority's own website has refused to load for us from several networks, so we describe its terms as reported by others and say plainly that we could not reach the source ourselves.

Re-score one of our rulings yourself

The mark-placement figures come from sittings where a person answered the same question over and over, and the answers are committed. One of those sittings can be scored again by anyone, from the repository and one hash-checked download, without taking our word for anything. Clone it, install, fetch the word pages the marks are read from, and run one command:

git clone https://github.com/omars-lab/hifth.git
cd hifth · pnpm install
pnpm --filter @hifth/etl build:words --fetch  (about 350 MB, checked against a committed hash)
make rescore RULING=2026-08-12T1650-placement-residual-by-hand.seed23

What you should see: a small table with three rows that matter — the fingerprint of the measurement the sitting was shown against, the fingerprint of the grading code, and the headline — each with a recorded column and a now column and the word same beside it; then the line everything agrees. The headline for that sitting is 98.3% [91.1% – 99.7%] 59/60: sixty placements over forty pages, fifty-nine of which the reader put nearer our correction than the box as first printed. The recorded column comes from a receipt committed beside the ruling, holding what the scorer said on the day — so you are comparing a published number with one your own machine just produced, not two runs on ours. If any row says DIFFERS, the command says which and why, and we would want to hear about it.

Two honest limits. The other rulings in that directory are settled tables, made from transcripts and a large measurement that were never committed; the same command refuses those in so many words rather than replaying something smaller, and make rescore on its own lists which is which. And nobody outside the project has run this yet — that is the half of the check we cannot do for ourselves.
