# What should a page-by-page reader take from other open-source apps? (researcher A, design reading)

Written 2026-11-16. This is my reading of the evidence in `2026-11-16-a-findings.md`, next to this file. Every app, file and line I lean on is listed there with how I checked it. Section numbers like "findings 1" point to that file.

A warning first. I read code; I did not run these apps or see them on a screen. So these are recommendations to build roughly and try by hand, not settled answers.

## What is the short version?

1. **Hide the bars; the page is the screen.** Show them on a tap (touch) or when the mouse reaches the edge (computer), and hide them again. Every paged reader I read does this.
2. **Navigation on the reading-start side, pens in the middle, search and "more" at the end** on a wide screen. On a tablet, make the pen strip something the reader can move.
3. **Split settings in two:** what changes the page you are looking at lives in a small menu on the reading screen; everything else lives in Settings. Make every setting something a message can take you to.
4. **Use three voices, each for one job:** a help line that follows the tool in hand, short messages that leave by themselves, and a message that stays only when the reader must act.
5. **No tour.** Hints that point at the real controls and vanish on first use, plus a hint at the moment of a hidden gesture.
6. **Two arrangements chosen by width, not one arrangement scaled.** Decide two-page view by the shape of the space, not the device name.
7. **Two link forms for every place, one parser, pages counted from 1.** Links that go somewhere just go; links that do something ask first.
8. **Put "back to where I was" next to the slider.** Make the slider run in the book's direction.

Licence note: Readest, Zotero, Aidoku, Kiwix and Sefaria are GPL or AGPL. Everything I recommend from them is an idea to build ourselves, not code to copy.

## 1. Where should the controls for moving and for changing the view live?

**What the best apps do.**

- Readest hides both bars. On a computer the mouse reaching the bottom edge shows the bottom bar; on a phone a tap does (findings 1).
- Quran for iOS shows bars on a tap and hides them after 5 seconds (findings 3).
- Aidoku toggles bars on a tap and keeps the slider in the bottom bar (findings 5).
- pdf.js and the Zotero web reader keep one top bar always visible (findings 6, 2).

**Where they disagree.** Hidden or always there. The two that never hide (pdf.js, Zotero web) are computer-first tools for working on a document. The three that hide (Readest, Quran for iOS, Aidoku) are for reading. They also disagree on the trigger: hover, tap, or a timer.

**What I recommend.**

- Bars hidden by default on every device. Bottom bar for moving through the book (slider, page box, back). Top bar for the title of where you are and the "view" and "more" menus. Rests on Readest, Quran for iOS and Aidoku.
- On touch: tap the page margin to show, and hide on the next page turn or after a pause. Rests on Quran for iOS and Aidoku.
- On a computer: show when the mouse reaches the bottom or top edge, and also on a key. Do not rely on hover alone. Readest's issue 6999 shows hover at the screen edge failing for a real reader (findings 1).
- If a new control is added, lengthen the pause for a while. Quran for iOS did exactly this and said why in a comment (findings 3).

**What would change this.** If a rough build shows people who are revising want the slider always in sight, the always-visible bar of pdf.js is the other model.

## 2. Where should the pens live, next to navigation?

**What the best apps do.**

- The Zotero web reader: one top bar, navigation at the start, pens in the centre, search at the end (findings 2).
- pdf.js: one top bar, navigation left, zoom centre, tools right (findings 6).
- Zotero for iPad: a separate pen strip the reader drags to the leading edge, trailing edge, top, or pinned under the top bar, with dashed outlines showing where it can go (findings 2).
- Excalidraw: tools top-centre on a computer, in the bottom bar on a phone (findings 4).
- Readest: no standing pens; tools pop up on a text selection (findings 1).

**Where they disagree.** Whether pens share the navigation bar (Zotero web, pdf.js) or get their own strip (Zotero for iPad, Excalidraw on a phone). And whether pens are always out (Zotero, Excalidraw) or appear only when needed (Readest).

**What I recommend.**

- Pens are their own strip, apart from the navigation bar, and they stay out while a pen is in hand. We have a freehand "mark a mistake" pen, and a popup tied to selecting text (Readest's way) cannot serve that. Rests on Zotero for iPad and Excalidraw.
- On a computer: pens in the centre of the top bar, between navigation and search. Rests on the Zotero web reader.
- On an upright tablet: a strip on the side edge that the reader can move to the other side. This is the one I would build first to feel, because which side is right depends on the hand, and on a right-to-left book the page turns from the other edge. Rests on Zotero for iPad.
- On a phone: pens in the bottom bar, swapping places with navigation while a pen is in hand. Rests on Excalidraw's phone layout.
- Keep Readest's idea for the *selection* tools only (copy, share a verse): a small popup at the selection.

## 3. How should a long settings list be organised?

**What the best apps do.**

- Quran for iOS has two levels: an in-reader "more" menu with about ten things that change the page in front of you, and a Settings screen for the rest (findings 3). Aidoku does the same with a reader settings sheet (findings 5).
- Readest has nine tabs, reopens on the last one, and can be sent straight to one setting, which it scrolls to and highlights (findings 1).
- Kiwix opens the standard Mac settings window with tabs on the Mac and a sheet on iPhone, and can open scrolled to one item when an error needs it (findings 8).
- NetNewsWire keeps five tabs and moves rare switches out of the window entirely (findings 9).

**Where they disagree.** Whether to look like the system's Settings. Kiwix and NetNewsWire do on the Mac. Readest draws its own dialog, the same everywhere. None of them has anything near 47 groups; the largest I saw is nine tabs.

**What I recommend.**

- Two levels, as Quran for iOS and Aidoku do. Everyday view choices (one or two pages, zoom, which marks show) go in a small menu on the reading screen. The 47 groups go behind Settings.
- Inside Settings, group the 47 under no more than about nine headings. Nine is the most any app here carries (Readest), and its tab names already have to shrink to icons on a narrow window.
- Give every setting an address, and let any message, help line or link open Settings at that setting, highlighted. Rests on Readest and Kiwix. This also answers "can a step be linked to" for settings.
- Reopen where the reader left off. Rests on Readest.
- Do not copy NetNewsWire's hidden keys, but copy the idea: a rare switch that one person asked for goes under a last "Advanced" heading with a note of why it exists.
- On the Mac, also open Settings from the menu bar with the usual shortcut. Rests on Kiwix. Whether the Mac window should *look* like system Settings I cannot say from this evidence: the two native apps do, the one web-drawn app does not.

## 4. How should the app talk to the reader?

**What the best apps do.**

- Excalidraw keeps a help line that changes with the tool in hand, and shows fewer of them on a phone (findings 4).
- Excalidraw's short messages last 5 seconds unless told to stay, and then carry a close button (findings 4).
- Readest puts plain information mid-screen and success or error at the top, in the top corner on wide screens. A separate 2-second hint strip sits at the top (findings 1).
- Home Assistant shows an error when a link is not understood, and a confirm box before a link acts (findings 7).

**Where they disagree.** Where short messages appear: Readest uses the top; I did not verify Excalidraw's position.

**What I recommend.**

- A help line that follows the pen in hand, on computer and tablet only, with a setting to turn it off. Rests on Excalidraw.
- Short messages that leave by themselves in 2 to 5 seconds for things that need no action. Rests on Readest (2 seconds for hints) and Excalidraw (5 seconds).
- A message that stays, with a close button and one action, only when the reader must do something. Rests on Excalidraw's "stay" option and Kiwix's jump to the setting.
- Place messages at the top, away from the bottom bar and the reader's thumb. Rests on Readest.
- I found no evidence about device notifications in any app I read. For the revision calendar that is a gap, not a "no".

## 5. How should the app teach a first-time reader?

**What the best apps do.**

- Excalidraw: three hints pointing at the real menu, toolbar and help button, shown only on an empty canvas and gone the moment you pick a tool (findings 4).
- Readest: no welcome at all, but a hint that appears during a hidden gesture, because "Nothing else advertises the gesture" (findings 1).
- Quran for iOS: "what's new" screens per version, and a longer bar pause when buttons are new (findings 3).
- NetNewsWire, Kiwix, Aidoku, pdf.js: nothing found.

**Where they disagree.** They mostly do not: nobody here ships a step-by-step tour. The split is between a little (Excalidraw, Quran for iOS) and nothing.

**What I recommend.**

- No forced tour. Rests on the absence of one in all ten apps.
- On first open, show the bars and a few hints pointing at the real controls, and remove each hint the first time that control is used. Rests on Excalidraw. Our trigger cannot be "empty canvas", so use "this control has never been used".
- For each hidden gesture (hold to turn, edge grab), show a small sign at the moment the reader starts it. Rests on Readest.
- When a release adds a control, a "what's new" card, not a tour. Rests on Quran for iOS.
- A "show the hints again" switch in Settings and in Help. I have no source for this; it follows from making hints vanish.
- **Gap.** Because none of these apps has a tour, I have no evidence on checklists, on switching a tour off, or on linking to a step. If that matters, the next pass should read apps I dropped unopened, the Wikipedia iPhone app first.

## 6. How should one design change between Mac, iPad, iPhone and a browser tab?

**What the best apps do.**

- Readest: two arrangements, switched at 640 pixels wide (findings 1).
- Excalidraw: three named sizes (phone to 599, tablet 600 to 1180, desktop above), decided from the size of the drawing area, with a special rule for a phone on its side (findings 4).
- pdf.js: one arrangement that sheds items in a fixed order at 840, 750, 690 and 560 pixels, moving some into "more" (findings 6).
- Kiwix: three different arrangements, bottom bar on iPhone, sidebar on iPad, sidebar plus menu bar on Mac (findings 8).
- Sefaria: as many side-by-side panels as fit at 360 pixels each (findings 10).
- NetNewsWire: separate interfaces per device, on purpose, written down (findings 9).

**Where they disagree.** Scale one design (pdf.js) or build different arrangements (everyone else). Decide by device name (Quran for iOS's default) or by measured space (Excalidraw, Aidoku, Sefaria).

**What I recommend.**

- Different arrangements, not one scaled. Five of six apps that span sizes do this; the one that scales (pdf.js) has no phone layout worth copying.
- Name the sizes once, in one place, and decide from the space the reader actually has, not the device. A Mac window dragged narrow should get the phone arrangement. Rests on Excalidraw and Readest.
- Three sizes to start: narrow (about 600 and under), middle, wide. Rests on Excalidraw's numbers and Readest's 640.
- Two-page view by shape: on when the space is wider than tall and wide enough, with the reader able to force one or two. Rests on Aidoku (single, double, automatic) and Quran for iOS (wider than 900 points; on by default except on phones).
- On the Mac add what only a Mac has: menu-bar commands for every navigation action. Rests on Kiwix.

## 7. How should links open the app at a place or a feature?

**What the best apps do.**

- Readest: a custom-scheme link and an https link for the same place; one parser for both; the https one is the one to share; in a browser it lands on a page that decides what to do; old link shapes still work (findings 1).
- Zotero: `open-pdf` links with a page counted from 1 and an optional note, and an old add-on's link shape still accepted (findings 2).
- Quran for iOS: `quran://2/255`, the same on Android, with tests that refuse impossible values (findings 3).
- Home Assistant: the first word is the action; "go there" links just go; "do this" links ask first; unknown links get an error (findings 7).
- pdf.js and Sefaria: the address in the browser bar always says where you are (findings 6, 10).

**Where they disagree.** Very little. Quran for iOS has only the custom scheme; Readest has both.

**What I recommend.**

- Every place and feature gets both forms, built from one description and read by one parser. Share the https form. Rests on Readest.
- Count pages from 1 in links and convert inside. Name verses the way a person does (sura, verse). Rests on Zotero and Quran for iOS.
- Sort link actions into "go there" (a page, a verse, a feature panel) and "change something" (add a note, mark a mistake, change a setting). Confirm the second kind unless the link came from the app's own surfaces. Rests on Home Assistant.
- Say so when a link is not understood. Rests on Home Assistant.
- Never retire a link shape once it has been shared. Rests on Readest, Zotero and Kiwix, which all still read their old ones.
- In a browser tab, keep the address bar equal to the current page so copying it is a share. Rests on pdf.js and Sefaria.
- Test bad values. Rests on Quran for iOS.

## 8. What should a page-by-page reader get right?

**What the best apps do.**

- **A trail back.** Readest puts Go Back and Go Forward next to the slider, greyed when empty (findings 1). pdf.js records your place *before* a jump so the browser's Back returns you (findings 6). The Zotero web reader has a back-after-jump button at the start of its bar (findings 2).
- **The slider.** Aidoku runs it backward for right-to-left books, ticks as pages pass, and enlarges the handle while held (findings 5). Readest waits 100 milliseconds before moving the book and shows the page label while dragging (findings 1).
- **Two pages.** Aidoku: single, double, automatic by shape (findings 5). Quran for iOS: by width, default on for tablets (findings 3). pdf.js: lets the reader say whether page 1 stands alone (findings 6).
- **Turning.** Aidoku offers named tap-zone maps, shown as pictures, borrowed from an Android reader (findings 5). Readest shows a growing bar at the edge during a hold-to-turn (findings 1).
- **Full screen.** pdf.js hides its controls after 3 seconds and turns on a 50-pixel swipe (findings 6).

**Where they disagree.** Where the trail lives: in the app's own buttons (Readest, Zotero) or in the browser's Back (pdf.js).

**What I recommend.**

- Back and Forward buttons beside the slider, greyed when empty, on every device. In a browser tab, also record the place before each jump so the browser's Back does the same thing. Rests on Readest and pdf.js. For someone revising who hops to a look-alike verse and must return, this is the most useful single thing I found.
- The slider runs right-to-left, following the book and not the device language. Show the page and sura while dragging and move the book only after a short pause. Rests on Aidoku and Readest.
- Two-page view: one, two, or automatic, and a choice for which pages face each other. A printed mus'haf has a fixed answer to which pages face, so the choice may be unnecessary; pdf.js shows the question exists.
- Do not adopt tap zones for turning. On our page a tap on a verse already means something. Aidoku's pages have nothing to tap.
- Show a sign at the edge during any hold-to-turn gesture. Rests on Readest.

## What is the one build I would do first?

A rough bottom bar with the slider running right-to-left and a Back button beside it, hidden until a tap or the mouse reaches the edge, tried on a phone, an upright tablet and a Mac window. It tests questions 1, 6 and 8 at once, and it is where the apps I read agree most.
