# What should a page-by-page reader take from other open apps? My reading (researcher B)

Written 2026-11-16. The evidence behind every line here is in [the raw findings](2026-11-16-b-findings.md), with links to the exact code read.
This is one researcher's reading. A second researcher worked separately, and a third person will compare the two.

## What is the short version?

- **Hide the bars while reading, and bring both back with one tap on the page.** Every reader I read does this. They differ only in how the bars go away again.
- **Use two arrangements, not one scaled layout.** A wide window gets one thin row with everything in it. A narrow window gets a few icons that each open a panel. Choose by the size of the window, not by the kind of device.
- **Give the pens their own zone, apart from page controls, and let the reader move it.** No app I read mixes pens in among the page buttons.
- **After any jump, offer a way back, and do not let the jump overwrite the reader's place.** This is the most useful single idea I found, and four apps do it four ways.
- **Keep everyday view choices next to the page. Put the long settings list somewhere else, grouped under about ten plain names.** Truly rare settings need no screen at all.
- **Teach with one short message and reopenable help, not a forced tour.** The tours I found all need a Skip button and rules for when to stop asking.
- **Publish every link in two forms: a private one and an ordinary web address.** The web address works even when the app is not installed.
- Copyleft warning: Readest, Zotero, Kavita, Aidoku and Kiwix are GPL or AGPL. We take ideas from them, never code.

## 1. Where should the controls for moving through the book live?

**What the best apps do.** They show the page alone and bring bars in when asked.

| App | How bars appear | How bars leave | Where the page control is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Readest | Computer: pointer at top or bottom edge. Phone: tap the page | Pointer leaves; tap again | Bottom |
| Kavita | Tap the middle of the page | A timer, paused while the slider is held; can be switched off | Bottom |
| Komga | Tap the middle of the page | Tap again | Bottom |
| Quran iOS | Tap the page | A 5-second timer after opening; tap again | Not verified |
| Open-Mushaf | Tap the page | Tap again, or the full-screen button | Top menu and a row of page numbers |
| Quran.com web | Always there | Hides on scrolling down, returns on scrolling up | A strip under the top bar |
| Zotero, PDF.js | Always there | Never | Top bar, left side |

**Where they disagree.** Book and comic readers hide everything and put the slider at the bottom. Document tools (Zotero, PDF.js) keep one bar always showing at the top. The split follows the job: people reading for pleasure want the page alone; people working on a document want tools in reach.

**What I recommend.**

- Hide both bars while reading, and toggle them with a tap on the page. (Readest, Kavita, Komga, Quran iOS, Open-Mushaf.)
- Keep the page slider at the bottom on every size. All four readers with a slider put it there, and it is where a thumb rests.
- On a computer, also show the bars when the pointer reaches the top or bottom edge. (Readest.) A computer reader should not have to click to find the controls.
- If bars close on a timer, pause the timer while the reader is using a control, and let the reader switch the timer off. (Kavita.)
- When the buttons change in an update, leave the bars up longer the first time. (Quran iOS wrote this reason into its code.)
- Show where the reader is in words (surah, juz, page) in the top bar, not only as a slider position. (Quran.com web, Open-Mushaf.)

**What would not carry over.** Hiding the bar on scroll (Quran.com web) needs a scrolling page, and ours does not scroll. An always-visible bar (Zotero, PDF.js) costs page height that a phone-sized mus'haf page cannot spare, though it is reasonable on a wide computer window while the pens are in use.

## 2. Where should the pens live, relative to the page controls?

**What the best apps do.**

| App | Where the pens are | Can the reader move them? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zotero, in the shared reader | Centre zone of the top bar, page controls on the left | No |
| Zotero on iPad | A separate bar: left edge, right edge, top, or pinned under the title | Yes, by long-press and drag; remembered |
| PDF.js | Right zone of the top bar; each pen opens its own small options panel below it | No |
| Excalidraw | Phone: bottom bar. Wider: floating at top centre | No, it follows window size |
| Readest | No standing bar; a small popup at the selected words | The popup's buttons can be chosen |

**Where they disagree.** Tools for working (Zotero, PDF.js, Excalidraw) keep pens on screen all the time. The reading app (Readest) shows them only when words are selected. Nobody puts a pen between two page buttons.

**What I recommend.**

- Give pens a zone of their own, visibly apart from page controls. (Zotero, PDF.js.)
- Pressing the pen already in hand puts it down. (Zotero.) This removes the need for a separate "stop" button.
- Put each pen's options (colour, kind of mistake) in a small panel that hangs off that pen, not in settings. (PDF.js, Zotero's colour button.)
- On a phone, put pens at the bottom within thumb reach; on wider screens, at the top or along an edge. (Excalidraw.)
- Let the reader choose which edge the pen bar sits on, and remember it. (Zotero on iPad.) This matters more for us than for most: a right-to-left book and left-handed readers both change which edge is out of the way.

**What would not carry over.** Readest's popup-at-selection assumes the reader selects running text. Marking a mistake on a printed page is a tap or stroke on a fixed spot, which suits a pen held in hand better than a popup.

## 3. How should a long list of settings be organised?

**What the best apps do.**

| App | Shape | Count at the top level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readest | Icon tabs; opens on the tab last used; a switch for "all books or this one" | 9 |
| Bluesky | A short list of plain names, each a page of its own | About 11 |
| NetNewsWire on iPhone | A grouped list like the system's Settings; first row opens the system's own Settings | 6 sections |
| NetNewsWire on Mac | A window with tabs | 5 |
| Home Assistant | One list of groups; on Mac it becomes a sidebar with the chosen group beside it | About 25 |
| Quran iOS | A small menu on the reading screen for view choices; fuller settings in a tab | About 8 rows |
| Kavita | View settings unfold under the slider, in place | About 10 |
| PDF.js | A "more" menu for rare view choices | About 15 |
| Quran.com web | A drawer with three tabs, a live preview of a verse, a reset button | 3 |

**Where they disagree.** Two families. One mimics the system's Settings: a list you drill into (NetNewsWire, Bluesky, Home Assistant). The other keeps settings on top of the page being read, so the reader sees the change as they make it (Kavita, Quran iOS, Quran.com web, Readest). Nobody I read shows more than about 25 groups at one level, and most show about ten.

**What I recommend.**

- Split the list in two. View choices a reader changes while reading (one or two pages, zoom, colour, which marks show) go in a small panel on the reading screen, with the page visible behind it. (Kavita, Quran iOS, Quran.com web.)
- Everything else goes in a settings screen grouped under about ten plain names. Forty-seven groups at one level is far outside what any app here does. (Bluesky, NetNewsWire, Readest.)
- Same groups, different frame per device: a drill-in list on a phone, a sidebar with the detail beside it on a wide window. (Home Assistant, NetNewsWire.)
- Reopen settings where the reader last was. (Readest.)
- Hide a row that does not apply on this device or in this mode, instead of showing it greyed out. (Quran iOS, Home Assistant.)
- Give rare settings no screen. If only one or two people would ever change it, keep the setting but leave it out of the list. (NetNewsWire wrote this down as policy.) Our rule that a losing design option stays as a setting makes this the natural home for most of them: one "experiments" group, or reachable only by link.
- Offer a reset. (Quran.com web.)

**What would not carry over.** Readest's "this book or all books" switch has no meaning with one book. Handing the reader to the system's own Settings (NetNewsWire) works only in the native shell, not in a browser tab.

## 4. How should the app talk to the reader?

**What the best apps do.**

| App | Short messages | Anything standing? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readest | 5 seconds; plain news in the middle, errors at the top | A 2-second hint in the page's own header strip |
| Excalidraw | 5 seconds, with a way to keep one open | One line of help that changes with the tool in hand |
| Bluesky | 3 seconds; bottom left on the web | No |
| Komga | 3 to 4 seconds | No |
| Kavita | Short messages for one-time teaching and for silent changes | No |

**Where they agree.** Three to five seconds. And the best uses are the same two: telling the reader about a change the app made on its own, and asking for a second press before something big.

**What I recommend.**

- Short messages last 3 to 5 seconds and never need closing. (All five.)
- Say so whenever the app changes something by itself. Kavita says "switched to single page, not enough room for two". Komga says which reading direction it picked and why. A reader who rotates a tablet and loses the second page should be told once.
- Use "press again to confirm" in place of a dialog for leaving the edge of the book. (Komga.)
- A standing help line is worth having only while a pen is in hand, where it says what the pen will do. (Excalidraw.) I found no reader that keeps a help line up during plain reading.
- Do not place messages over the slider or the pens.

**What I did not find.** No app I read uses device notifications for reading. I did not examine notification code in any of them, so this is a gap, not a finding. The revision calendar is the one feature of ours that would want them.

## 5. How should the app teach itself on first run?

**What the best apps do.**

| App | What it does | Can it be skipped? | Can it be reopened? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Readest | Nothing found | | |
| Kavita | One short message the first time a book is opened, with the bars open | It goes by itself | No |
| Open-Mushaf | A few swipeable slides at first launch | Yes, a Skip button | Yes, it is its own screen |
| Quran.com web | A tour pointing at real buttons, in four named groups, with a checklist | Yes | Yes, any group from the checklist |
| Wikipedia iOS | One tip bubble on one button, with rules for when | Closing retires it for good | No |
| Excalidraw | Hand-drawn arrows on the empty canvas | They vanish at the first stroke | They return whenever the canvas is empty |
| Quran iOS | "What's new" for updates only; new installs see nothing | | |

**Where they disagree.** Completely. This is the question with the least agreement. The largest reader here (Readest) teaches nothing. The two Qur'an apps for the general public both teach, one with slides and one with a tour.

**What I recommend.**

- Start with the least: one short message on first open that names the one hidden thing, "tap the page for the controls". (Kavita.) Everything else in the app can be found once the bars are showing.
- Put a reopenable help screen behind a button, made of short steps, each with its own address so it can be linked to. (Open-Mushaf's tutorial is its own screen. Quran.com's tour can start at any group.) Our app already opens at a place from a link, so a link to a help step costs little.
- Teach a feature at the moment it first matters, one tip at a time, and never while something else is on screen. (Wikipedia.) The first jump is the moment to point at "back".
- If there is ever a checklist, cap how often it asks. Quran.com asks once a month, three times at most, then stops for good.
- Never show old "what's new" notes to someone who installed today. (Quran iOS.)
- Flash the tap areas briefly after the reader changes how tapping works, with a setting to turn that off. (Kavita.)

**What would not carry over.** Excalidraw's arrows on an empty canvas need an empty state, and a mus'haf page is never empty. A forced tour suits a public website with first-time visitors better than a tool for people who already know the book by heart.

## 6. How should one design adapt across Mac, iPad, iPhone and a browser tab?

**What the best apps do.**

| App | How it decides | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Excalidraw | Size of its own drawing area: phone under 600 px, tablet to 1180 px, desktop above | Where the tools sit |
| Readest | 640 px, wide or tall | A full bottom row, or five icons opening panels |
| Bluesky | Three widths | Bottom bar; icon-only side strip; full side list |
| Kiwix | The width class the system reports | Bottom bar, or a sidebar beside the content |
| PDF.js | Steps at about 690 and 560 px | Same bar, buttons drop out |
| Open-Mushaf | None; one column at most 640 px wide | Almost nothing |
| Aidoku | Wider than tall | One page or two |
| Quran iOS | Wider than 900 points and not a phone | One page or two |

**Where they agree.** Nobody who supports phones and computers well scales one layout. They keep the same parts and rearrange them. And the better ones decide by the space they actually have, not by the device's name.

**What I recommend.**

- Decide by the size of the window the app has, not by device. A Mac window dragged narrow, an iPad sharing its screen with another app, and a phone should all get the narrow arrangement. (Excalidraw, Kiwix, Readest.)
- Name three sizes. Two is too few: Bluesky, Excalidraw and Kiwix all needed a middle one for tablets.
- Narrow: a bottom row of a few icons, each opening a panel above it. Wide: one row with the controls laid out in full. (Readest.)
- Two pages when the window is wider than it is tall and wide enough, re-decided on rotation or resize, keeping the reader's page. Offer "one page", "two pages" and "automatic". (Aidoku, Quran iOS.) Say so when automatic changes it. (Kavita.)
- Test the middle size hardest. Kiwix's change notes show the iPad arrangement breaking again and again: bars that vanished, a sidebar that forgot its state.

**What would not carry over.** Open-Mushaf's single 640 px column is honest and simple, but it gives up the two-page spread, which is the natural way to see a printed mus'haf on a wide screen. Dropping buttons as PDF.js does works only while few buttons are needed; it removed "next page" on small screens, which we could not accept.

## 7. How should links open the app at a place or a feature?

**What the best apps do.**

| App | Private link | Ordinary web address too? | Worth copying |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Readest | Yes | Yes; opens the app on phones, a web page on computers | Shared notes use the web form |
| Bluesky | Yes | Yes | Same |
| Wikipedia iOS | Yes | Yes | A public table of every link, with examples |
| Quran iOS | Yes | Not verified | Forgiving reading of the link; same format as its Android app |
| Zotero iOS | Yes | Not verified | Pages in links count from 1, whatever the code does inside |
| Home Assistant | Yes | Yes, on its own redirect site | A link that does something asks first |
| PDF.js | No | Yes; page and zoom ride in the address | Works with nothing installed |

**Where they agree.** The apps that ship on both web and phone all give the same place two addresses, and hand out the ordinary web one.

**What I recommend.**

- Every place has an ordinary web address that works in a browser with nothing installed, and opens the app when it is installed. Shared verses and help steps use this form. (Readest, Bluesky, Wikipedia.)
- Keep the private form for other apps and shortcuts on the same device.
- Numbers in links mean what a reader means: page 1 is the first page; a verse is surah then verse number. (Zotero, Quran iOS.)
- Read links forgivingly, and match the format other Qur'an apps already answer where we can. (Quran iOS matched its Android sibling on purpose.)
- A link may show anything without asking. A link that would change something (add a mark, change a setting, send something) asks the reader first. (Home Assistant.)
- Publish one page listing every link the app answers, each with an example. (Wikipedia.)

**What would not carry over.** Home Assistant's long list of link kinds serves automation, which is beyond what a reader needs.

## 8. What do page-by-page readers do well?

**The trail back after a jump.** Four apps, four designs:

| App | What the reader sees | The rule behind it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open-Mushaf | A strip: "Return to reading position (page N)", plus a button to make the new page the saved place. Shows only while away | A jump is marked temporary and does not overwrite the saved page |
| Zotero | A back arrow in the top bar, greyed out when there is nowhere to go | Moves less than 2 seconds apart count as one; scrolling never counts; a jump always counts |
| Readest | Back and forward arrows beside the slider | Not read in detail |
| PDF.js | The browser's own back button | Passing positions are replaced, settled ones kept |

I recommend both halves. Keep one saved place that only deliberate reading moves, and show a "return to page N" strip while the reader is away from it, with a way to adopt the new page. (Open-Mushaf.) Under it, keep a short trail so that several jumps in a row can be undone one at a time, using Zotero's rule so that dragging the slider through fifty pages leaves one step and not fifty. In a browser tab, the browser's back button should walk the same trail. (PDF.js.) Look-alike verses make this matter more for us than for anyone here: the reader hops to compare and must get back to exactly where they were.

**The slider.**

- For a right-to-left book the slider runs right to left. (Aidoku builds this in. Readest swaps its arrows.)
- The thumb grows while held and can be caught a little outside its edge. (Aidoku.)
- Show the page the thumb is over while dragging, and the current and last page numbers at the ends. (Readest, Komga.)
- Put a type-a-number box beside it. (Readest, Zotero, PDF.js.) A slider alone is too coarse for 604 pages on a phone; every app with a long document pairs the two.
- Follow the finger at once and settle half a second after release. (Zotero.)

**Page turning.**

- Tap the left or right side to turn, the middle for the bars; let the reader change this. (Kavita, Aidoku.)
- A turn that reads as a sheet lifting, with the page moving only slightly. (Open-Mushaf's design note.) On a two-page spread, the outer page turns as a leaf hinged at the spine. (Readest's design note.)
- At the first or last page, a second press is needed before leaving. (Komga.)

**Two pages and full screen.**

- One, two or automatic, as in question 6. Kavita also offers "two pages, reversed", which shows that the side each page falls on needs a deliberate rule in a right-to-left book.
- Full screen is a button in the bars, and in full screen the bars still come back with a tap. (Kavita, Open-Mushaf.)
- Keep the screen awake while a page is open. (Quran iOS.)

## Which three finds matter most?

1. **A jump should never cost the reader their place.** A saved place that jumps do not overwrite, a visible "return to page N", and a trail that merges quick moves. (Open-Mushaf, Zotero, Readest, PDF.js.)
2. **Rearrange by the size of the window, in three sizes, and say so when the app changes the view by itself.** (Excalidraw, Readest, Kiwix, Aidoku, Kavita.)
3. **Everyday view choices live on the page; the long list is about ten groups; rare settings get no screen.** (Kavita, Quran iOS, Bluesky, NetNewsWire.)

## What would change these answers?

- Seeing the apps run. I read code and project documents only. A layout that reads well in code can feel wrong in the hand, and this project's own rule is that a rough build finds what a list cannot.
- Design discussions I did not look for. If Readest or Zotero explained in an issue why the slider is where it is, that would outweigh my reading of their code.
- The other researcher finding an app that ships on all three platforms and disagrees with Readest.

## What did I not manage to look at?

- No app was run, and no web version was opened at computer or phone width. Nothing here was checked by eye.
- Issue trackers, forums and blog posts were not searched for the reasons behind layouts. The "why" material is only what sits inside each repository.
- Store listings were not opened, so which apps are really offered for Mac is taken from each README.
- Device notifications were not examined in any app.
- Problem reporting, optional sign-in and each project's public site were only noticed in passing.
- Quran iOS, Quran.com web and Home Assistant were read from a few files each. Whether Quran iOS has a page slider is not verified.
- Whether Kavita's slider flips for right-to-left books is not verified.
- Pen-first apps (a handwriting notes app, a desktop PDF annotator) were listed and dropped unread. Question 2 rests on three apps and would gain most from more looking.
- The licence of the Quran.com web code is unclear and needs checking before anything beyond an idea is taken from it.
