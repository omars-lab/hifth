# Slanting the book's transliterated terms by their marks: right 99 times in 100

**2026-10-14.** The book sets some words of its notes in italics. Neither the capture nor the
text read off the page pictures records which. The notes within two taps of the demo had their
slanted words read off the pictures by hand, and those show them in italics. Every note further
out showed all its words upright, and a scholar in the room might open any verse.

An earlier note on this ([slanting a note's quotation of its own verse](italics-from-quoted-verse-words.md))
said the other italics, terms and Arabic words among them, "cannot be found from the words".
For one kind they can.

## What was tried

The book writes an Arabic word in English letters with marks no English word has: a line over a
long vowel (ā, ī, ū), a dot under a letter (ḥ, ṣ, ṭ, ẓ, ḍ), and the signs for ʿayn and hamzah.
The rule: a word with one of those marks and no capital letter anywhere in it is slanted. A
capital marks a name (a person, a place, a commentator's initial), and the book sets names
upright. That includes a name after "al-", so a capital anywhere in the word, not only the first
letter, keeps it upright.

## What the evidence showed

Measured against the notes read by hand (1,012 notes, 4,290 slanted runs), counting words, not
quoting any:

- 997 places hold a word the rule picks. The book slants 983 of them, so the rule is right 99
  times in 100.
- They are 435 different words. 432 are slanted every time they appear. One is upright every
  time, and two are slanted in most places and upright in a few.
- Counting every marked word, with or without a capital, the rule would be right only 42 times
  in 100. Counting a word as a name only when its first letter is a capital, 78 times in 100. Only
  "a capital anywhere" leaves the names out.
- It finds a quarter of the slanted runs (1,069 of 4,290). The other three quarters are English
  words: a quotation of the verse, a term glossed in English, a book's title, or a transliteration
  the book writes without marks. The rule cannot see those.

The notes nobody read hold 4,197 such words. They are 1,489 different words, and 39 in 100 of
those places hold a word already seen in the hand-read notes, always slanted. The rest are new
words. The 99 in 100 is a fact about the book's habit, not about a list of words, so it should
carry over, but it was not measured there.

Two notes nobody read were looked at on their page pictures: every word the rule picks is
slanted in print. Both pages also slant words the rule does not pick (an unmarked
transliteration, and a capitalised name of God given in Arabic), as the count above expects.

## What replaced it

The rule now runs in the pitch's notes build, after the hand-read rows. It touches only the
notes nobody read: every note of a verse with a hand-read row is left as the rows made it,
under every verse that shares it. A read note keeps exactly what the reader saw, including its
one-in-a-hundred upright marked words.

The rule is in the italics step of the notes build, with its tests on made-up words. The pitch
test opens one note well beyond two taps and checks that its marked terms slant.

## What is still upright

Beyond two taps of the demo, quoted verse words, English glosses, titles and unmarked
transliterations still show upright: about three slanted runs in four. Reading the slant off
the page pictures themselves is still what would bring those back.
